Meet π,
the number that never ends.

Take any circle. Divide the distance around it by the distance across it. You always get the same number: 3.14159… It’s true for a coin, a pizza and the planet Earth.

The red line is the circle’s edge, unrolled. It fits 3 whole widths and a bit more. That “bit” is 0.14159…, and it goes on forever.

So what is π, really?

π = C ÷ d

C is the circumference (distance around). d is the diameter (distance across through the center). Their ratio is always π.

It’s a ratio

Big circle or tiny circle, the ratio doesn’t change. That’s why π is a constant, one fixed number for every circle in the universe.

It’s irrational

You can’t write π as a fraction of two whole numbers. Its decimals never end and never settle into a repeating pattern.

It’s transcendental

Even stronger: π is not the answer to any polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients. This was proved in 1882, and it means you can’t “square the circle” with just a ruler and compass.

A 4,000-year chase

  • About 1900 BCE
    Babylonians use 3.125. Egyptians use about 3.16. Close enough to build with.
  • About 250 BCE
    Archimedes traps π between 3 10⁄71 and 3 1⁄7 by squeezing a circle between two 96-sided shapes.
  • About 480 CE
    Zu Chongzhi in China finds 355⁄113, correct to 6 decimals. It stayed the best estimate for nearly 900 years.
  • 1400s, Kerala, India
    Madhava of Sangamagrama and the Kerala school discover infinite series for π, a big step toward modern calculus: π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …
  • 1706 and 1737
    William Jones first uses the Greek letter π. Leonhard Euler makes it famous, and everyone follows.
  • Today
    Computers have calculated π to over 100 trillion digits. Not because we need that many, but to test supercomputers and algorithms.

The first 50 digits

NASA uses only about 15 or 16 digits for space navigation. With 40 digits, you could measure the whole visible universe to the width of a hydrogen atom.

Beat your memory

Where π shows up (not only circles)

Waves and sound

Music, radio and light are waves, and waves are built from sines and cosines. π is baked into them.

Rivers

The winding length of a river divided by its straight-line distance tends to average close to π.

Dropping needles

Toss needles on a lined floor. The share that cross a line lets you estimate π. It’s called Buffon’s needle.

Your body

Eyes, the DNA helix and the heart’s rhythms are all studied with equations that contain π.

Pi Day

Celebrated every March 14 (3/14). Albert Einstein was born that day in 1879.

Fun fact

The Greek letter π was picked because it’s the first letter of perimetros, the Greek word for “perimeter.”
